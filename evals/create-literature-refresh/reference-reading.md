## Transferable rules for the refresh

These are recommendations inferred from the observations, not assertions about the authors’ intentions.

1. **Keep category identity vivid at the boundary or centerline.** If a fill or interval is softened, preserve a clear group-colored edge/curve/marker. Blue/coral hollow bars in scWAT Fig. 2 and blue/red curves in Vanneste Fig. 3b demonstrate different ways to do this.
2. **Give different layers different jobs and weights.** A summary curve may need to be wider than a cloud of points; a black comparison line may need to be stronger than a colored bar edge. Do not reduce all widths together. Vanneste Fig. 5d supplies a particularly clear dense-point example.
3. **Choose filled or hollow marks from overlap and the information to display.** Open circles remain distinguishable over hollow bars (Vanneste Fig. 2e–h); tiny filled dots suit large point fields (Fig. 4a,b). Hollow is not automatically superior.
4. **Use a second visual channel when there are two grouping variables.** Combine hue with open/filled interior, shape, or a restrained hatch; explain both channels in the legend. scWAT Fig. 3g and Fig. 5b,c are concrete examples.
5. **Build density with alignment and spacing.** Keep related small plots regular, pack within-category marks, and leave a larger boundary between categories or blocks. Share truly common labels/legends. Vanneste Fig. 5d and scWAT Fig. 3g show dense arrangements with an explicit reading order.
6. **Retain crisp distribution boundaries when the shape itself carries information.** Colored violin area, a black contour, and a visible inner summary can coexist, as in PROGENy Fig. 4c. Check their separability at final display size instead of prescribing a pale fill alone.
7. **Reserve space for the whole legend and the annotation layer.** A dot-size legend needs visible size differences and labels as well as its keys; a colorbar needs tick/label clearance. Comparison lines need headroom above data. Vanneste Fig. 4e and scWAT Fig. 4f make these footprints visible.
8. **Match separators and grids to geometry.** Small, discrete matrix cells can benefit from white separators (PROGENy Fig. 3b); thousands of heatmap cells can become a coherent texture without individual borders (Vanneste Fig. 4d). Keep a useful grid behind the marks when present; absence of a grid is also legitimate.
9. **Use gray for a specific secondary role, and label selectively.** Gray can distinguish a background point population or neutral summary; it should not dull every category. Use data-grounded selected labels and leader lines where necessary (PROGENy Fig. 3a, 5b; Vanneste Fig. 8e).

## Limits and required data meanings

- **Observed:** these papers contain saturated colors, definite black strokes, hollow and filled bars, many points, crowded but regular small multiples, and several chart-specific continuous palettes. They do not exhibit one uniform “light/minimal” style.
- **Inferred:** the user’s preferred crispness can plausibly be reproduced through boundary contrast, stroke hierarchy, structured spacing, and preserved color identity. This needs final-size checking in the actual target panel.
- **Unknown:** exact palettes, font files, original line widths, export settings, and whether any observed color selection passes an accessibility test. Some tiny labels are readable only after enlargement. The figures alone cannot establish numerical accuracy, sample independence, interval calculations, filtering, fitting, or statistical validity.
- Before implementation, the real dataset must identify category order, grouping variables, individual observations versus summaries, interval meaning, any legitimate size encoding, and the meaning/range of continuous values. No source measurements or statistical conclusions should be reconstructed from the images.
